{\color{ctublue}\LARGE\bfseries How to learn the talk with this booklet}

{\color{ctublue}\rule{\textwidth}{1pt}}

\medskip
The booklet has three parts, each with one page per slide. Work through them in order,
slide by slide, always \textbf{aloud}, standing if you can.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Learn the skeleton, not the words.} Per slide, learn verbatim only the
        \emph{opening sentence}, the \emph{last sentence} (it is the bridge to the next
        slide) and the \emph{numbers}. Everything in between is a list of points you say
        in your own words. A talk recited word for word sounds recited and collapses on
        the first slip; anchor sentences give you a safe place to land.
  \item \textbf{Part A -- full text.} One sentence per line; every line break is a pause.
        Read the slide twice, then cover the text and say it while looking at the thumbnail.
  \item \textbf{Part B -- first letters.} Say the slide from the skeleton. Peek only when
        stuck. Move on once you manage two clean runs.
  \item \textbf{Part C -- anchors only.} Say the whole slide from the opening and closing
        sentence alone. This is the level you need on stage.
  \item \textbf{Time it.} Each page carries its time window. The pauses are part of the
        timing. Target for the whole talk: \textbf{9:30}.
  \item \textbf{Space it out.} Day 1: slides 1--5 to Part C. Day 2: slides 6--11, then re-test
        1--5. Day 3: two full runs with the PDF full-screen and a clicker, one of them recorded
        on your phone -- listen back. Day 4: open the booklet on a random page and start
        from there (this trains recovery from a blank), plus the questions in \texttt{QA.md}.
        Last evening: one relaxed run. \textbf{Freeze the script two days before the talk};
        every edit resets what you have learnt.
  \item \textbf{Anki.} \texttt{anki.csv} holds one card per slide, one card per bridge
        sentence and one per number -- for the minutes between sessions.
\end{enumerate}

\slidehead{1}{Title}{0:00–0:45}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{1}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Good morning.\par
I'm Martin Maxa from the Department of Measurement, here at this faculty.\par
This is joint work with Jakub Svatoš from the same department.\par
Today I'll talk about detecting, classifying and localizing impulsive sounds – in our case, gunshots.\par
The main part is gunshot classification, where we compare different cepstral features and two classifiers.\par
And I'll briefly show how the system localizes the source.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{2}{Motivation and research questions}{0:45–1:50}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{3}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This work is part of a larger project – an acoustic sensor system for protecting soft targets.\par
Acoustic detection is a good complement to cameras.\par
Sound is omnidirectional, and you don't need a line of sight.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}The full system has to detect an impulsive event, classify it – ideally down to the calibre – and localize the source.}\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape And it has to work outdoors, with noise and a lot of reflections.\cutclose{}}\par
\click
In this study we ask three questions.\par
First, which cepstral features best separate gunshots from similar impulsive sounds?\par
Second, which classifier works better – a neural network or a support vector machine?\par
And third, how does the frame length affect the results?\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{3}{The acoustic signature of a gunshot}{1:50–3:00}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{5}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Let's start with the signal of a gunshot.\par
A gunshot has one or two components, depending on the projectile speed.\par
A subsonic shot has only the muzzle blast.\par
A supersonic one also has a shock wave.\par
This recording is a supersonic shot, so we can see both.\par
The first one, at about two and a half milliseconds, is the shock wave.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}It's created along the bullet's path and has the typical N-shape.\cutclose{}}\par
\click
The second one, about forty-five milliseconds later, is the muzzle blast.\par
It comes from the gases leaving the barrel, and every gunshot has it.\par
Everything between and after them is mostly reflections from the surroundings.\par
This matters for the frame length, and I'll come back to it in the results.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{4}{Single-domain analysis is insufficient}{3:00–3:30}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{6}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This also shows why one domain alone is not enough.\par
The spectrum often tells you more about the environment than about the gunshot.\par
And the waveform changes as the sound travels and hits obstacles.\par
So we need features that combine both domains, and cepstral features do exactly that.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{5}{Proposed system architecture}{3:30–4:20}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{7}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Now to the system itself.\par
It's a set of independent sensor units connected to a remote server.\par
Each unit continuously monitors its surroundings.\par
When it detects an impulsive event, it sends the recorded signal to the server.\par
The server then does two things – it classifies the event and localizes the source.\par
More units mean wider coverage, and they let us estimate the position of the source.\par
We tested the system with several calibres and types of ammunition.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{6}{Localization – bearing from TDoA}{4:20–5:20}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{9}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Let me briefly explain the localization first.\par
Each sensor unit has two MEMS microphones, about twenty centimetres apart, sampled at 44.1 kilohertz.\par
We measure the time difference of arrival between the two microphones.\par
From this delay we get the direction to the source, using the equation here.\par
\click
So one unit gives us one direction.\par
With two units we get two directions, and where they cross is the position of the source.\par
We tested this on seventy events at nine reference angles.\par
With cross-correlation and parabolic interpolation, the mean absolute error was zero point seven seven degrees.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{7}{Feature extraction – four cepstral representations}{5:20–5:55}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{10}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Now to the main part – classification.\par
We compared four cepstral feature extractors.\par
They differ in the filter bank they use.\par
MFCC uses the mel scale, with dense filters at low frequencies.\par
It comes from speech processing, but it works for gunshots too.\par
LFCC has linearly spaced filters, and IMFCC uses an inverted mel scale, dense at high frequencies.\par
GTCC uses a gammatone filter bank, which is based on a model of human hearing.\par
It's also reported to be more robust to noise than MFCC.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{8}{Feature extraction – the processing chain}{5:55–6:25}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{11}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
And this is how the features are computed.\par
All four use the same chain – framing, FFT, the filter bank, then a logarithm and a DCT.\par
The filter bank is the only place where they differ, and here you can see all four of them on the same frequency axis.\par
The coefficients then go to a classifier.\par
We tested two – a neural network and a support vector machine – with three frame lengths, fifteen, thirty and fifty milliseconds.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{9}{Classification results – 9 mm class}{6:25–6:40}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{12}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This is the full table of results for the nine-millimetre class.\par
The GTCC rows are highlighted.\par
I won't go through the numbers – let's look at the same data as a chart.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{10}{Classification results – MCC at a glance}{6:40–7:35}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{13}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Here are the same results, just the Matthews correlation coefficient.\par
MCC takes the whole confusion matrix into account, so it's less affected by class imbalance than accuracy.\par
The red line is GTCC.\par
It's on top in both panels, at every frame length.\par
The neural network does better than the SVM, and the gap grows with longer frames.\par
The best configuration is GTCC with the neural network and a fifty-millisecond frame.\par
That gives ninety-nine point one five percent accuracy, ninety-six point seven seven percent recall, and an MCC of zero point nine eight.\par
In other words, one hundred and seventeen correct decisions out of one hundred and eighteen.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{11}{Discussion}{7:35–8:45}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{15}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Three things to take from this.\par
First, MFCC, LFCC and IMFCC give similar results, so the filter spacing makes little difference.\par
Second, GTCC is clearly better.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}One likely reason is that the gammatone bank comes from a hearing model and is known to be more robust to noise.}\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape But we didn't test that directly.\cutclose{}}\par
Third, the neural network is slightly better than the SVM, especially with longer frames.\par
That's a general trend, not true for every single result.\par
\click
And finally, longer frames generally improve accuracy.\par
But at fifty milliseconds, the frame already contains more and more reflections, not just the gunshot itself.\par
So even longer frames wouldn't necessarily help.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{12}{Conclusion}{8:45–9:30}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{16}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
To sum up.\par
We presented a distributed acoustic sensor system that detects, classifies and localizes impulsive sounds.\par
For gunshot classification, cepstral features work well with both classifiers.\par
GTCC gives the best results of the four.\par
And the neural network is slightly better than the SVM, especially with longer frames.\par
All of this was tested on real firearms, with several calibres and types of ammunition.\par
Thank you.\par
}
