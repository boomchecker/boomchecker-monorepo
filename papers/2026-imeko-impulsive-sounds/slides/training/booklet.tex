{\color{ctublue}\LARGE\bfseries How to learn the talk with this booklet}

{\color{ctublue}\rule{\textwidth}{1pt}}

\medskip
The booklet has three parts, each with one page per slide. Work through them in order,
slide by slide, always \textbf{aloud}, standing if you can.

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=4pt]
  \item \textbf{Learn the skeleton, not the words.} Per slide, learn verbatim only the
        \emph{opening sentence}, the \emph{last sentence} (it is the bridge to the next
        slide) and the \emph{numbers}. Everything in between is a list of points you say
        in your own words. A talk recited word for word sounds recited and collapses on
        the first slip; anchor sentences give you a safe place to land.
  \item \textbf{Part A -- full text.} One sentence per line; every line break is a pause.
        Read the slide twice, then cover the text and say it while looking at the thumbnail.
  \item \textbf{Part B -- first letters.} Say the slide from the skeleton. Peek only when
        stuck. Move on once you manage two clean runs.
  \item \textbf{Part C -- anchors only.} Say the whole slide from the opening and closing
        sentence alone. This is the level you need on stage.
  \item \textbf{Time it.} Each page carries its time window. The pauses are part of the
        timing. Target for the whole talk: \textbf{9:30}.
  \item \textbf{Space it out.} Day 1: slides 1--5 to Part C. Day 2: slides 6--11, then re-test
        1--5. Day 3: two full runs with the PDF full-screen and a clicker, one of them recorded
        on your phone -- listen back. Day 4: open the booklet on a random page and start
        from there (this trains recovery from a blank), plus the questions in \texttt{QA.md}.
        Last evening: one relaxed run. \textbf{Freeze the script two days before the talk};
        every edit resets what you have learnt.
  \item \textbf{Anki.} \texttt{anki.csv} holds one card per slide, one card per bridge
        sentence and one per number -- for the minutes between sessions.
\end{enumerate}

\slidehead{1}{Title}{0:00–0:45}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{1}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Good morning.\par
My name is Martin Maxa, from the Department of Measurement here at this faculty.\par
I worked on this together with Jakub Svatoš, from the same department.\par
Today I will talk about acoustic detection, classification and localization of impulsive sounds, with gunshots as the target class.\par
The main focus is gunshot classification: we compare different cepstral features and two classifiers.\par
I will also briefly show the localization.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{2}{Motivation and research questions}{0:45–1:50}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{3}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This work is part of the development of an acoustic sensor system for the protection of soft targets.\par
Acoustic detection can complement camera-based surveillance.\par
Sound is omnidirectional, and it does not need a direct line of sight.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}The complete system should detect an impulsive event, classify it, ideally down to the calibre, and localize its source.}\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape It should also work outdoors, where there is noise and many reflections.\cutclose{}}\par
\click
We focus on three questions.\par
First, which cepstral representation works best for separating gunshots from similar impulsive sounds?\par
Second, which classifier performs better: a neural network or a support vector machine?\par
And third, how does the frame length affect the results?\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{3}{The acoustic signature of a gunshot}{1:50–3:00}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{5}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Let us first look at the signal of a gunshot.\par
A gunshot has one or two components, depending on the projectile speed.\par
A subsonic shot has only the muzzle blast; a supersonic projectile also produces a shock wave.\par
This recording is a supersonic shot, so we see both.\par
The first component, at about two and a half milliseconds, is the shock wave.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}It is produced along the projectile trajectory and has a characteristic N-shaped pressure profile.\cutclose{}}\par
\click
The second one, about forty-five milliseconds later, is the muzzle blast.\par
It comes from the gases at the barrel and is present in every gunshot.\par
Between and after these two components, we mainly see reflections from the surroundings.\par
They matter for the choice of frame length, and I will return to them in the results.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{4}{Single-domain analysis is insufficient}{3:00–3:30}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{6}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This also shows why one signal domain alone is not enough.\par
The spectrum often describes the environment more than the gunshot itself.\par
The time-domain shape changes during propagation and because of obstacles.\par
So we need features that combine both domains, and cepstral features do exactly that.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{5}{Proposed system architecture}{3:30–4:20}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{7}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Now let us look at the proposed system.\par
It consists of several independent sensor units connected to a remote server.\par
Each sensor continuously monitors its surroundings.\par
When it detects an impulsive event, it sends the recorded signal to the server.\par
The server then performs two main tasks: classification of the event and localization of the sound source.\par
Several units give wider coverage and allow us to estimate the source position.\par
The system was tested with several firearm calibres and ammunition types.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{6}{Localization – bearing from TDoA}{4:20–5:20}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{9}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
First, I will briefly explain the localization.\par
Each sensor unit has two MEMS microphones, about twenty centimetres apart, sampled at 44.1 kilohertz.\par
We measure the time difference of arrival between the two microphones.\par
From this delay, we can calculate the direction to the source using the equation shown here.\par
\click
One sensor unit therefore gives us one direction.\par
With two sensor units, we get two directions, and their intersection gives us the estimated source position.\par
We tested this on seventy events at nine reference angles.\par
Using cross-correlation with parabolic interpolation, we achieved a mean absolute error of zero point seven seven degrees.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{7}{Feature extraction – cepstral coefficients}{5:20–5:50}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{10}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
Now I will move to the main part of the work: classification.\par
We compared four different cepstral feature extractors.\par
All of them use the same chain: framing, FFT, a filter bank, and then a logarithm and a discrete cosine transform.\par
The main difference is the filter bank.\par
You can see all four filter banks here, drawn on the same frequency axis.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{8}{Feature extraction – the four filter banks}{5:50–6:25}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{11}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
MFCC uses the mel scale, with dense filters at low frequencies.\par
It comes from speech processing, but it also works for gunshots.\par
LFCC uses linearly spaced filters, while IMFCC uses an inverted mel scale, with dense filters at high frequencies.\par
GTCC uses a gammatone filter bank based on a model of the human auditory system.\par
It is also reported to be more robust to noise than MFCC.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{9}{Classification results – 9 mm class}{6:25–6:40}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{12}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This table shows the complete classification results for the nine-millimetre class.\par
The GTCC rows are highlighted.\par
I will not go through the numbers here – let us look at the same data in a chart.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{10}{Classification results – MCC at a glance}{6:40–7:35}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{13}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
This chart shows the same results, but only the Matthews correlation coefficient.\par
MCC considers the whole confusion matrix, so it is less affected by class imbalance than accuracy.\par
The red line is GTCC.\par
It is on top in both panels and for every frame length.\par
The neural network gives better results than the SVM, and the difference grows with longer frames.\par
The best configuration is GTCC with the neural network and a fifty-millisecond frame.\par
It reaches an accuracy of ninety-nine point one five percent, a recall of ninety-six point seven seven percent, and an MCC of zero point nine eight.\par
That is one hundred and seventeen correct decisions out of one hundred and eighteen test events.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{11}{Discussion}{7:35–8:45}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{15}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
From these results, we can make three main observations.\par
First, MFCC, LFCC and IMFCC give similar results, so the filter spacing makes little difference.\par
Second, GTCC performs clearly better.\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape \cutopen{}One possible reason is that the gammatone filter bank is based on an auditory model and is known to be more robust to noise.}\par
{\color{ink!55}\itshape However, we did not study this mechanism directly.\cutclose{}}\par
Third, the neural network performs slightly better than the SVM, especially with longer frames.\par
This is a general trend, not true for every single result.\par
\click
Finally, longer frames generally improve the classification accuracy.\par
But with a fifty-millisecond frame, we already start to include more reflections from the environment, not only the gunshot itself.\par
So even longer frames would not necessarily help.\par
}
\newpage
\slidehead{12}{Conclusion}{8:45–9:30}{Part A -- full text}
\thumb{16}
{\Large\raggedright\sloppy\setlength{\parskip}{6pt}
To conclude.\par
We presented a distributed acoustic sensor system for the detection, classification and localization of impulsive sounds.\par
For gunshot classification, cepstral features work well with both tested classifiers.\par
Among the tested features, GTCC gives the best results.\par
The neural network also performs slightly better than the SVM, especially with longer frames.\par
The system was evaluated on real firearms with several calibres and ammunition types.\par
Thank you.\par
}
